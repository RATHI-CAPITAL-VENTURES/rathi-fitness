Checklist for each day

The day is a list, and the list is the workout.

One live row, never two.

Four done, one working, one waiting — and only the working row gets the accent rail and a colour. A list where every row competes is a list you re-read every time you look down at it between sets. Tapping the live row is what opens the set screen.

The meta line carries what actually happened.

4 × 8 · 185 lb · all four hit and last set was 5 — the plan and the deviation in the same breath. A checklist that only shows the plan makes you open four screens to find out how last week went.

Body weight lives at the bottom of Today.

Not its own tab. You weigh yourself once, in the morning, and then you want it out of the way — but it belongs to the day, so it sits under the day. The number is Fraunces; it is one of the two things on this screen you are actually here for.

Six-forty-two in the morning.

The status bar time is set to when you actually train. Small thing, but a mockup that says 9:41 is a mockup of Apple's day, not yours.

Cooldown is a temperature, not a number in the corner.

The screen is the timer.

Between sets you are holding a phone at arm's length, out of breath, and you need one thing: am I going yet. So the clock is 230 points across, the room glows the colour of the clock, and you can read it without focusing. The weight you are about to lift sits below it, because that is the second question, not the first.

Ember while you recover, teal when you're ready.

The ring starts hot the instant you rack the bar and cools through amber to teal, arriving only in the last quarter of the rest. That late handoff is deliberate — a gradual mood ring tells you nothing, but a colour that changes decisively at 0:20 is a signal you catch from your peripheral vision. This is the direct port of RIA's "her state is the accent," and it is the one risky idea in the set.

Set pips, not "Set 4 of 4".

Four dashes, three filled. The count is also written in the nav bar for when you want the words, but the shape is what you read mid-workout.

Plate math, in plate colours.

185 means a red 45 and a yellow 25 a side. The colours are the ones on real bumper plates, and they appear nowhere else in the app — they are not a palette, they are a lookup table you already know by sight.

The button tells the truth about time.

It says Skip to set 4, not Next — you are cutting a rest short, and the app should say so rather than pretend you finished it. It is also outlined while the clock runs and goes solid teal at 0:00: the loudest thing on screen should never be the button that undoes the thing the screen is for.

The last three sessions, not just the last one.

One prior session tells you what you did; three tell you whether you are stalling. 180 · 8, 8, 7, 6 twice running is the reason 185 is on the bar today, and that decision should be visible at the moment you make it rather than two taps away in Trends.

Weight, and trends over time

Two weights, one screen, because they are the same question.

Body weight and working weight, stacked.

You asked for "current weight, see trends over time," which is ambiguous on purpose — it means both the number on the scale and the number on the bar. Splitting them into two tabs makes you flip between them to answer one question, so the scale gets the chart and the lifts get a table underneath it.

The chart has an opinion.

An area fill under the line, a faint grid, and the last point called out by name. A sparkline with no endpoint marker makes you guess where today is, which is the only point you came to see. The trend rate — −0.65/wk — is stated in words rather than left for you to eyeball off a slope.

A dash is a real answer.

Deadlift shows —, not +0. It ends where it started, and the sparkline beside it shows the deload week that got it there — net nothing, but not nothing happened. A zero implies you measured a change and it came out zero, and those are different facts. Nothing turns green for standing still.

The risky idea

Watch it cool.

Ninety seconds of rest, compressed to about nine so you can see the whole arc. The ring holds ember for the first three quarters, then hands over to teal in the final stretch — the moment you can catch without looking straight at it. On the phone the room glow behind the timer does the same thing, so the change is visible with the screen face-down-adjacent on a bench, in your peripheral vision, mid-conversation.

If you hate it, it's one function.

The whole mechanic is a hue ramp over the rest fraction. Rejecting it costs a constant — the ring, the timer, and the layout all survive with a fixed accent. That is why it's the risk I picked: it is loud and it is cheap to undo.

It also has to work on the wrist eventually.

Colour survives a shrink to a 45mm face where a progress arc and a number do not, which is a second argument for encoding the state in hue rather than in geometry.

How RIA sees any of this

The phone owns the data. RIA reads a copy.

You chose CloudKit, which is right for a bag-and-basement app that has to work with no signal. The catch is that a CloudKit private database cannot be read from a Mac command line — there is no app context to authenticate as, so gym today would have nothing to talk to. The fix costs one background task: the app also writes a snapshot into an iCloud Drive container, which on macOS is just files sitting on disk that sync themselves.
